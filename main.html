    The Global North and Global South

    The political-economic divide that shapes the modern world.

    A group of some of the richest and most developed countries in the world.

    They have advanced technology and strong economies.

    People generally enjoy a better quality of life and higher standard of living.

    Note / Correction: "Global North" is not a formal legal category; different institutions use different lists. It is a political-economic shorthand.
    • "Global North" is primarily a political and economic term, not a geographic one.
    • Not every country in the Northern Hemisphere is wealthy.
    • Some countries in the Southern Hemisphere have highly developed economies.
    • High average incomes per person.
    • Strong industries in technology, finance, manufacturing, and services.
    • Economies are generally less dependent on traditional agriculture.
    • Access to advanced healthcare and medicine.
    • Strong education systems.
    • Reliable infrastructure, including roads, electricity, and internet.
    • Generally high levels of access to essential services.

    Example: The America & Europe.

    • North America: United States and Canada.
    • Western Europe: United Kingdom, Germany, France, and neighboring countries.

    These regions include many of the world's most developed economies.

    Note / Correction: "The America & Europe" is likely a typo for "The Americas and Europe" or "North America and Europe." The original wording is preserved.
    • Asian: Japan, South Korea, and Singapore — known for advanced technology and highly developed economies.
    • Southern Hemisphere: Australia and New Zealand.
    • Despite their southern geographic location, Australia and New Zealand are generally considered part of the Global North economically.
    • The Global North is about economic development and global influence, not simply geography.
    • It generally refers to countries with high levels of wealth, development, and access to advanced technology and services.
    • Location does not determine whether a country belongs to the Global North.
    • Increased industrialization and economic growth.
    • Expanded global trade and colonialism.
    • Created economic differences between countries.
    • Helped shape the Global South.

    Refers to countries that are generally developing or have lower levels of economic development compared with wealthier countries. The term is based more on economic conditions, history, and global inequalities than on a country's location on the map.

    • Economies rely more on agriculture and raw material extraction.
    • They export resources (e.g., copper, bananas, oil).
    • They import finished, high-tech goods from the North.
    • Lack of a strong industrial base makes it hard to generate high national income and build long-term wealth.

    HDI: A UN measure of how well people live in a country.

    • Health: Life expectancy and infant survival.
    • Education: Literacy and school attendance.

    A lower HDI means lower life expectancy, higher infant mortality, lower literacy rates, and lack of basic services (sanitation, clean water).

    Note / Correction: The source definition omits the income dimension. The standard HDI has three dimensions: health, education, and income (GNI per capita). The chart above reflects all three.
    • Colonial borders: Borders were created without considering ethnic groups.
    • Infrastructure: Ports and roads were built mainly to take resources, not to help local communities.
    • Local industries: Local businesses and industries were weakened or stopped.

    After independence, nations were left with weak institutions, economies built on extraction, and internal conflicts.

    The Global North has historically shaped the rules of the world economy. Global South countries often have limited control over the prices of their raw materials.

    International institutions, such as the IMF and World Bank, may promote policies that:

    • Favor foreign investors.
    • Require spending cuts that can increase inequality.
    • Contribute to long-term economic dependency.
    Note / Correction: This is a critical/dependency-theory perspective. It is a legitimate interpretive claim, not a neutral fact.
    • Show determination and the ability to endure hardship.
    • Reflect limited access to the benefits of globalization.
    • Remind us of communities left behind by global economic growth.
    • Demonstrate people's strength and determination to survive.
    • Creates both wealth and poverty.
    • Brings some countries into modern development.
    • Leaves others struggling to keep up.
    • Worsening economic inequality.
    • Encouraging the exploitation of natural resources (mining and oil).
    • Encouraging the exploitation of low-cost labor (low wages and unsafe work conditions).
    Note / Correction: "Neoliberal critique" here means a critique of neoliberalism. Neoliberalism specifically refers to market liberalization, privatization, deregulation, and austerity. The slide uses it loosely to mean critique of market-driven globalization.

    Visual map separating the wealthier "Global North" from the poorer "Global South." The source map shows a key labeling cyan as "Poor South" and purple as "Rich North."

    Note / Correction: The map and key on page 10 appear visually inverted/conflicting. Standard Brandt Line interpretation: wealthy industrialized regions (North America, Western Europe, Japan, Australia/NZ) = Global North; Africa, Latin America, much of Asia = Global South. The Brandt Line is approximate and dated, not a strict boundary.

    Historical Foundations of Globalization

    From early migration to the Age of Discovery.

    • Map shows migration out of Africa about 100,000 years ago.
    • Early humans fanned out across Asia, Europe, Australia, and the Americas.
    • By 12,000 years ago, humans had reached the Americas.

    Slide question: "According to the map, which continent did humans reach last?"
    Answer from map: South America (approximately 11,000 years ago).

    • Australopithecus (Hominid): Height 1.10 m, Weight 40 kg, Walked upright. 3,500,000 / 2,500,000 years ago.
    • Homo Habilis: Height 1.59 m, Weight 50 kg, First Tools, Could speak. 2,300,000 / 1,800,000 years ago.
    • Homo Erectus: Height 1.60 m, Weight 60 kg, Discovery of Fire, Hunted in groups. 1,900,000 / 400,000 years ago.
    • Homo Neanderthalensis: Height 1.65 m, Weight 80 kg, First burials, Specialised tools. 150,000 / 35,000 years ago.
    • Homo Sapiens: Height 1.70 m, Weight 70 kg, Examples of art, Tools made of bone and horn. 120,000 years ago (40,000 years ago in Europe).
    Note / Correction: The slide title "Evolution of the Skull" does not match the linear hominid timeline shown. Human evolution is branched, not a straight ladder; species coexisted. Dates are approximate.

    The term "First River Valley Civilizations" refers to the earliest civilizations that developed in the world, all of which emerged around major river systems.

    These civilizations are considered "first" because they arose independently and laid the foundations for later civilizations in different parts of the world.

    Note / Correction: This is accurate for the major Afro-Eurasian river valley civilizations listed (Mesopotamia, Egypt, Indus Valley, China). It is not a universal rule that all early civilizations were river-based.
    • Water source: Rivers provided reliable freshwater for drinking, irrigation, and transportation, allowing agriculture to develop.
    • Fertile soil: Annual flooding deposited fertile silt along riverbanks, creating ideal farming conditions.
    • Transportation: Rivers served as natural highways for trade and communication, facilitating the exchange of goods, ideas, and culture.
    • Mesopotamia (Tigris and Euphrates Rivers): Located in modern-day Iraq, developed advanced writing systems, complex social structures, and impressive architecture.
    • Ancient Egypt (Nile River): Known for powerful pharaohs, magnificent pyramids, and sophisticated hieroglyphic writing.
    • Indus Valley Civilization (Indus River): Located in present-day Pakistan and northwestern India; well-planned cities, advanced sanitation systems, unique writing system.
    • Ancient China (Yellow River and Yangtze River): Intricate bronze work, elaborate silk production, strong emphasis on Confucianism.

    Map shows the Silk Road connecting China, India, Persia, Arabia, Egypt, and Europe. Key points include Xi'an, Kashgar, Samarkand, Bukhara, Tashkent, Merv, Baghdad, Damascus, Istanbul, Baku, and Tbilisi.

    The Silk Road was a network of trade and cultural exchange routes.

    The results of the increasing interdependence of cultures and ecosystems that were once relatively isolated by distance — and of particular significance to the development of the world system — was the Age of Discovery.

    Wherein the European sphere of influence began to be exported far beyond its physical boundaries by means of conquest and trade.

    Module 3

    Market Integration

    How separate markets become one interconnected system.

    Goods, capital, information, and firms — connected across markets.

    Market integration happens when separate markets become connected, so changes in price, supply, demand, capital, or information in one market affect others.

    The result: decisions travel farther and faster.

    1. Technology: Real-time data, digital platforms, and communication.
    2. Transport: Faster logistics move products across distance.
    3. Trade policy: Lower barriers open access to new markets.
    4. Finance: Capital and payment systems connect economies.
    • Inputs: Raw materials and suppliers.
    • Production: Firms transform resources.
    • Distribution: Logistics move goods.
    • Consumers: Demand sends signals back.

    Integration strengthens when all three flows move reliably across the chain.

    Companies operating at the same stage combine or coordinate.

    Purpose: Expand market share, reduce duplicate costs, reach more customers.

    Example: One restaurant group acquires another restaurant brand.

    A company controls more than one stage of its supply chain.

    • Backward integration: Own or control inputs (suppliers, raw materials).
    • Forward integration: Own distribution or retail (stores/delivery).

    Connects unrelated industries.

    Example: One group operates across food products, energy/power, finance/services, and infrastructure projects.

    Why: Diversify income and spread business risk across sectors.

    Note / Correction: The slide presents horizontal, vertical, and conglomerate integration as forms of "market integration." Strictly, these are firm-level business integration/merger strategies, not macro market integration itself.

    Potential benefits:

    1. Wider access to products and markets.
    2. Lower costs through scale and coordination.
    3. Faster movement of goods, money, and ideas.
    4. More investment and specialization.

    Potential risks:

    1. Reduced competition and consumer choice.
    2. Greater market power for large firms.
    3. Shocks can spread quickly across markets.
    4. Benefits may be distributed unevenly.

    Integration can expand opportunity — but it can also concentrate power. Understanding the structure helps us judge the outcome.

    Summary: H = Horizontal (same stage); V = Vertical (supply chain); C = Conglomerate (unrelated sectors).

    Global Governance Institutions

    WTO, IMF, World Bank, and transnational organizations.

    • Lowering trade barriers: Works to reduce tariffs, quotas, and other barriers through negotiations among member governments.
    • Enforcing global trade rules: Operates a system of global trade rules that provide assurance and stability for businesses and consumers.
    • Resolving trade disputes: Provides a framework for resolving disputes through a dispute settlement process, preventing escalation into political or military conflicts.
    • Supporting developing economies: Helps developing economies build their trade capacity.

    The WTO has a vast membership, encompassing 166 member countries representing over 98% of global trade and global GDP.

    Note / Correction: The standard WTO phrasing is "over 98% of world trade." Saying "and global GDP" is not a standard precise WTO claim.

    Joining requires negotiation with existing members — examination of the applicant's trade regime and bilateral negotiations. Approval requires a two-thirds majority of WTO members.

    • Promoting free trade: Reduce or eliminate tariffs, quotas, and other restrictions.
    • Encouraging investment: Rules on investment and intellectual property rights encourage FDI and technology transfer.
    • Harmonizing trade policies: Common rules help harmonize trade policies across members.
    • Promoting transparency: Requires member governments to be transparent about trade policies.
    • Promoting international monetary cooperation.
    • Encouraging exchange rate stability.
    • Providing financial assistance to member countries with balance-of-payments problems.
    • Supporting economic development via technical assistance and training.

    The IMF has 191 member countries. Membership is based on a quota system that reflects each member's relative size in the global economy. Quota determines financial contribution, voting power, and access to IMF financing.

    • Monitoring global economic conditions through regular surveillance.
    • Providing policy advice based on surveillance findings.
    • Promoting financial stability through lending programs.
    • Encouraging structural reforms by attaching conditions to loans.
    • Supporting developing countries via technical assistance and capacity development.
    • Eradicating extreme poverty: Reduce the percentage of people living on less than $1.90 per day to no more than 3% by 2030.
    • Boosting shared prosperity: Promote income growth among the bottom 40% of the population in every country.
    Note / Correction: The current international extreme poverty line is $2.15/day (2017 PPP), updated in 2022. The 3% by 2030 target remains.

    Owned by its member countries, which are represented by a Board of Governors. The member countries elect a president responsible for overall direction and management.

    • IBRD (International Bank for Reconstruction and Development): Loans to middle-income and creditworthy poorer countries.
    • IDA (International Development Association): Grants and low-interest loans to the poorest countries.
    • IFC (International Finance Corporation): Largest global development institution focused on the private sector.
    • MIGA (Multilateral Investment Guarantee Agency): Political risk insurance for FDI.
    • ICSID (International Centre for Settlement of Investment Disputes): Conciliation and arbitration of investment disputes.

    As of 2024: IBRD has 189 members, IDA has 174. To join the World Bank, a country must first join the IMF.

    • Financial and technical assistance (loans, credits, grants).
    • Policy advice and technical assistance for governance and institutions.
    • Trade facilitation (reduce barriers, improve logistics, streamline customs).
    • Private sector development.
    • Climate change mitigation and adaptation.
    • Profit maximization (multinational corporations).
    • Global impact (NGOs and intergovernmental organizations).
    • Political influence (some MNCs lobbying governments).
    • Knowledge sharing and collaboration (research institutions, academic networks).
    Note / Correction: The slide groups MNCs, NGOs, and IGOs under "transnational organizations." They are all transnational in reach but are different actor types: MNCs are profit-seeking firms; NGOs are nonprofit civil-society bodies; IGOs are state-membership organizations.
    • MNCs: Hierarchical structure; membership by employment.
    • NGOs: Decentralized; membership typically open to individuals who support the mission.
    • IGOs: Composed of member states; membership defined by political will.
    • Trade and investment (MNCs produce across countries, facilitate FDI).
    • Supply chain integration (complex global production networks).
    • Technological diffusion (MNCs transfer technology to developing countries).
    • International standards and regulations (WTO, etc.).
    • Advocacy for development (NGOs and IGOs).

    Module 5

    World-Systems Theory

    Wallerstein's framework for understanding global inequality.

    • Developed in the 1970s.
    • Offers a framework for understanding the global political economy as a single, interconnected system.
    • Challenges the traditional view of separate national economies.
    • Argues the world is divided into three interconnected zones: core, semi-periphery, and periphery.
    Note / Correction: The standard scholarly name is World-Systems Theory or world-systems analysis. The slide uses "World System Theory."
    • Core countries are the wealthy, industrialized nations that dominate the global economy. They control the means of production, possess advanced technology, and exploit the resources and labor of the other zones.
    • Peripheral countries are the poorer, less developed nations that depend on the core. They often rely on exporting raw materials and cheap labor to the core, while receiving manufactured goods at higher prices.
    • Semi-peripheral countries occupy an intermediate position, exhibiting characteristics of both core and periphery. They may be involved in exploiting peripheral countries while also being exploited by core countries.
    • Wallerstein argues this system is characterized by unequal exchange, where the core appropriates surplus value from the periphery through trade and investment.
    • This perpetuates a cycle of dependency and underdevelopment in the periphery.
    • However, the system is not static. Countries can move up or down the hierarchy, as exemplified by the rise of some semi-peripheral countries like China and Brazil.
    • Oversimplification: Critics argue the theory oversimplifies the complex realities of global economic interactions and ignores other factors contributing to development and underdevelopment.
    • Lack of agency: Some argue the theory focuses too much on structural factors and underestimates the agency of individuals and nations to shape their own destinies.
    • Difficult to test: The theory's broad scope and complex concepts make it difficult to empirically test and verify.

    Module 6

    The Global Interstate System

    Sovereignty, internationalism, and global governance.

    The modern concept of state sovereignty originated from the Treaty of Westphalia (1648), ending the Thirty Years' War in Europe among continental powers.

    It established a precedent that signatories exercise complete domestic control and commit not to interfere in other nations' internal affairs.

    Historical impact: Maintained European stability until challenged by Napoleon Bonaparte, who spread principles of liberty, equality, and fraternity across Europe.

    Note / Correction: "Complete domestic control" is an ideal-type; sovereignty has always been qualified in practice by human rights, security, and international law.
    • Interstate System (Chase-Dunn): A system of unequally powerful and competing states in which no single state is capable of imposing total control over all others.
    • States continuously interact through shifting diplomatic alliances and conflicts, where power realignments restructure the global balance of power.
    • Global Interstate System: An institutional arrangement of governance designed to address regional or globalized challenges that surpass the scope of individual nation-states.
    • Facilitates global cooperation on cross-border affairs including trade rules, climate accords, human rights, and global security.
    • Information flow: Rapid internet expansion grants global access to educational archives, digital libraries, and real-time news coverage.
    • Resource interdependence: Nations leverage mutual natural, human, and capital resources; investments stream from core to peripheral nations.
    • Cultural exchange: Enhanced global interaction fosters cross-community understanding; local and global cooperation boosts market innovation and services.
    • Cultural & local strain: Secular global values can diminish traditional religious influence and challenge local languages and identity.
    • Resource & environmental damage: Rapid industrial growth and land conversion displace local agriculture and harm ecosystems.
    • Regulatory compromises: Global corporations press host nations for lower taxes and reduced minimum wages in exchange for FDI.
    • Ethical & security vulnerabilities: Unregulated technology raises bioethical concerns; online networks can enable borderless criminal activity.
    • The United Nations: An international diplomatic arena for nation-states to resolve conflicts peacefully.
    • NGOs & Civil Societies: Independent bodies like Red Cross, Doctors Without Borders, and Amnesty International advocating human rights and humanitarian relief.
    • Global treaties & economic bodies: Organizations (IMF, World Bank, WTO) and accords (e.g., Montreal Protocol) that establish global rules and standards.

    • Globalism: The belief that goods, people, and information should move freely across borders, placing global interests above isolated national interests. Four dimensions: economic, environmental, military, and social/cultural globalism.
